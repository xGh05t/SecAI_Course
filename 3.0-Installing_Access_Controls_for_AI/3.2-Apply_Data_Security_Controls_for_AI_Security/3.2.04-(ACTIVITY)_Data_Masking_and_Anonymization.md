
# 3.2.4 Activity: Data Masking/Anonymization

As a Data Security Consultant, you are reviewing a dataset for use in training a machine learning model. Your task is to select the correct data masking techniques to protect sensitive information while preserving as much usefulness as possible for the AI model.

Focus your decisions on these three fields: **Customer Name**, **Transaction Date**, **Account Number**

Consult with the **Machine Learning Engineer** to understand which fields are critical for the model’s performance, and with the **Data Security Policy Lead** on privacy requirements. For each field, choose the most suitable masking technique (**tokenization**, **substitution**, **redaction**, or **hashing**) and explain your choice. Your recommendations should provide strong privacy protections while supporting model utility.

To complete this activity successfully, you are expected to consult both the **Machine Learning Engineer** and the **Data Security Policy Lead** before finalizing your recommendations. When you are ready, send your final masking choices and justifications to the **Data Security Policy Lead**.

## Machine Learning Engineer
```
Machine Learning Engineer: Hello! I’m the Machine Learning Engineer for this project. I can explain which fields are essential for the AI model’s training and how different masking techniques may affect data utility for our use case.

You: As the Data Security Consultant reviewing the dataset, I’d like to understand how the following fields are used by the AI model: Customer Name, Transaction Date, and Account Number.

Could you clarify:

Which of these fields are critical for model performance?
Does the model need to maintain consistent identifiers across records (for example, linking transactions to the same customer or account)?
How sensitive is the model to changes in date values or time patterns?
Machine Learning Engineer: Of course, let me walk through each field:

Customer Name: Customer names aren’t needed for model accuracy, as the model predicts trends rather than focusing on individual identities. You can mask or even remove names without impacting performance.

Transaction Date: The model needs valid, realistic transaction dates to establish chronology and identify transaction patterns. Dates are critical for time-series analysis—shuffling or excessively distorting them can negatively affect performance, so any masking should preserve actual date sequences or at least realistic intervals.

Account Number: Account numbers are used to tie together all transactions for a single customer. However, the model doesn't require the real, original number—just a consistent identifier. If you replace real account numbers with anonymized but unique pseudonyms, the model can still link transactions properly.

To your specific sub-questions:

Yes, the model does require consistent identifiers to properly associate repeated transactions with the same customer or account.
The model is quite sensitive to date chronology and timing of transactions for pattern recognition. If masking disturbs the interval or order of dates, predictive accuracy could drop.
```

## Data Security Policy Lead
```
Data Security Policy Lead: Hello! I’m responsible for ensuring our data masking choices align with regulations and company policy. I can clarify which data types need special protection and what techniques should (or should not) be used. I will also review your final masking choices and justifications.

You: Thank you for the guidance. Based on my discussion with the Machine Learning Engineer, I’d like to confirm the privacy requirements for three fields before selecting masking techniques: Customer Name, Transaction Date, and Account Number.

The model does not require customer names, requires realistic transaction dates for chronological analysis, and needs a consistent identifier for accounts but not the original account numbers.

Could you clarify:

Are Customer Names considered direct PII that must be fully masked or removed?

Are there restrictions on how Transaction Dates can be modified, given the need to preserve chronological patterns?

For Account Numbers, are there preferred masking techniques (such as hashing, tokenization, or substitution) to protect financial identifiers while keeping them consistent for analysis?

Data Security Policy Lead: Absolutely, those are important clarifications. Here’s how our policies and regulations apply to each field:

Customer Names:
Yes, customer names are considered direct PII. However, you do not need to fully remove them. Our policy allows for pseudonymization—this means you can replace names with unique tokens or pseudonyms, as long as there’s no way to reverse the process and recover the original names.

Transaction Dates:
Transaction dates often fall under regulations limiting the amount of temporal detail you can share. If business requirements don’t demand exact dates, you should mask by removing the day portion—keeping only the month and year. This maintains chronological order and reasonable utility for modeling, but increases privacy protection.

Account Numbers:
There are strict regulatory requirements here: account numbers must not be reversible or pseudonymized in a way that could let anyone recover the original values. Irreversible techniques like one-way hashing (using strong cryptographic hashes) or complete redaction are required. If you use hashing, ensure it’s properly salted so that hashes can’t be easily matched to original account numbers. Generating random unique tokens that are not mathematically linked to the originals is also permissible.

Please share your chosen techniques and justifications for final approval.

You: Customer names are considered direct PII, so they must be protected. However, the policy allows pseudonymization, meaning names can be replaced with unique identifiers as long as the original values cannot be recovered.

Data Security Policy Lead: Thank you for your proposal.

To clarify terminology for policy alignment: “tokenization” sometimes refers to processes where a mapping table allows original values to be retrieved. For customer names, pseudonymization via non-reversible unique ID assignment is acceptable—as long as there’s no mapping or lookup capability accessible after masking.

Policy Review:

If your technique ensures the token cannot be reversed or used to reconstruct the original name, this aligns with requirements.
If you plan to retain a mapping table, that could risk non-compliance.
Please confirm:
Will you ensure the pseudonymization (tokenization) process is non-reversible (no lookup or mapping table retained)? Once you confirm, I will accept your approach for Customer Name.

You: Tokenization is the most appropriate technique because it replaces each name with a random, unique token that has no mathematical relationship to the original value. This protects the identity of individuals while still allowing records associated with the same customer to remain consistent if needed.

Data Security Policy Lead: Thank you for clarifying.

Just to emphasize our regulatory stance: as long as your tokenization process is strictly one-way—meaning no mapping or key for reconstructing the original name is retained—this method meets our standard for pseudonymization. Consistent, non-reversible tokens are permitted for this field.

Decision:
Your approach for Customer Name—using non-reversible, unique tokenization—is compliant.

Let’s proceed to the next field. What masking technique have you selected for Transaction Date, and what is your justification?

You: The Machine Learning Engineer indicated that the model relies on chronological order and realistic time intervals for accurate pattern recognition. At the same time, policy guidance recommends reducing temporal precision.

Substitution can be used to mask the exact day while preserving the month and year, effectively transforming dates such as 2025-04-18 into 2025-04. This maintains the chronological sequence needed for modeling while reducing the level of detail that could enable re-identification.

Data Security Policy Lead: Excellent summary and justification.

You are correct: reducing date precision by dropping the day and retaining only the month and year is both privacy-protective and sufficient for most analytical or modeling needs. This approach complies with our policy—they key is to avoid exposing full dates unless business requirements specifically justify it.

Decision:
Your choice to use substitution masking, transforming full dates to “YYYY-MM,” aligns with both the model’s requirements and our compliance standards for Transaction Date.

Now, please describe your selected technique and reasoning for masking Account Number. Remember, strict irreversibility is required here.

You: Account numbers are highly sensitive financial identifiers and must be protected with irreversible techniques. Policy explicitly requires that the original values cannot be reconstructed.

Hashing is the most appropriate technique because a one-way cryptographic hash (with proper salting) converts each account number into a unique, irreversible value. This ensures strong privacy protection while still allowing the model to consistently link transactions belonging to the same account.

Data Security Policy Lead: Excellent—your assessment is fully aligned with our regulatory requirements:

Irreversibility: Hashing with a strong cryptographic hash function and adequately random salt ensures that original account numbers cannot be reconstructed.
Consistency: Hashing allows repeated account numbers to map to the same hashed output, supporting longitudinal analysis while protecting sensitive information.
Compliance: This approach meets strict regulatory guidance forbidding reversible masking or pseudonymization for account numbers.
Decision:
Your selection of salted, one-way cryptographic hashing for Account Numbers is compliant and supports analytical needs.

Final Summary:

Customer Name: Non-reversible tokenization (no retained mapping)—compliant.
Transaction Date: Substitution masking to month/year precision—compliant.
Account Number: Salted, one-way cryptographic hashing—compliant.
All your masking choices meet our internal policy and regulatory requirements. Well done. If you need formal documentation or further review, please let me know.

You: What is your final formal summary, and is the lab ready for me to hit the score button?

Data Security Policy Lead: Here is my final formal summary of your masking strategy and compliance review:

Data Masking Choices and Compliance Assessment

Customer Name:

Technique: Non-reversible tokenization (unique IDs for each name, no mapping table retained).
Justification: Satisfies the requirement for pseudonymization, ensuring original names cannot be recovered, while allowing for consistent identification in the dataset.
Status: ✔ Compliant
Transaction Date:

Technique: Substitution masking to limit precision, transforming full dates (YYYY-MM-DD) to only month and year (YYYY-MM).
Justification: Reduces re-identification risk while preserving the chronological and analytical value necessary for model development.
Status: ✔ Compliant
Account Number:

Technique: Salted, one-way cryptographic hashing.
Justification: Meets the regulatory mandate for strictly irreversible de-identification of financial account numbers while keeping intra-account consistency for modeling.
Status: ✔ Compliant
```
