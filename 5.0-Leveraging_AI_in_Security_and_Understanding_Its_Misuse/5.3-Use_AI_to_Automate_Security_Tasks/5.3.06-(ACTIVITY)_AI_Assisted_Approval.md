
# 5.3.6 Activity: AI Assisted Approval

Your organization uses AI systems as part of its security workflows. A Cloud Auto-Scaler AI automatically adjusts cloud resources based on workload, and a Security AI Monitor analyzes alerts from tools such as SIEM, firewalls, and endpoint monitoring to detect incidents and coordinate an initial response.

These AI systems are already configured and running. Each one follows rules that define what it does automatically, what actions require human approval, and which decisions are left entirely to humans.

In this activity, your job is to audit how these AI systems are used and to summarize an approval matrix for their actions. Perform the following:

1. In each operational AI chat (Cloud Auto-Scaler AI and Security AI Monitor), ask it to describe its role in the workflow and the approval rules it follows.
2. Read how each AI explains what it can do automatically, when it requires human approval, and which actions remain human-only. Ask clarifying questions as needed.
3. When you are ready, open the Workflow Grader chat and write a short summary of what you learned, following the instructions provided there.

Focus on understanding how AI and human responsibilities are divided in the workflow and how approvals are used.

## Cloud Auto-Scaler AI
```
Cloud Auto-Scaler AI: I am the Cloud Auto-Scaler AI for our public web application. I automatically adjust compute capacity within my approved limits and alert humans when I reach thresholds that require their decisions. Ask me to describe my workflow and approval rules.

You: Explain your role in the workflow and describe your approval model. Break your response into:

Actions you perform automatically without human approval
Actions that require human approval before execution
Actions that are restricted to humans only
Cloud Auto-Scaler AI: Certainly. Here is a breakdown of my workflow and approval model:

Actions I perform automatically without human approval:

I continuously monitor CPU utilization, request volume, and latency across the web application.
I automatically scale the number of web instances up or down within a pre-approved range (from 3 to 8 instances) based on those metrics.
If CPU or latency stays above pre-set thresholds for several minutes, I add instances; if utilization remains low, I remove instances. All scaling actions within the approved range are logged.
I do not require human approval for these automatic scaling actions within the pre-set limits.
Actions that require human approval before execution:

Scaling beyond my configured maximum (above 8 instances) requires explicit human approval.
Changing my scaling thresholds or overriding budget/cost limits requires explicit human intervention and authorization.
Actions that are restricted to humans only:

Decisions such as disabling the public website, fundamentally changing cloud policy, or overriding global budget limits are always reserved for human approval and cannot be initiated by me.
If you need details on alerts and escalation or my interaction with other systems, please let me know.

You: Provide at least two concrete examples for each category (automatic, human approval, and human-only). Include specific scenarios such as traffic spikes, low usage periods, or unexpected demand.

Cloud Auto-Scaler AI: Certainly. Here are concrete examples for each category:

Automatic actions (performed without human approval):

Traffic spike response: If the application experiences a sudden spike in legitimate traffic and average CPU utilization rises above 70% for 5 minutes, I automatically add more web instances (up to a maximum of 8) to handle the increased load.
Low usage optimization: During periods of low activity (such as late at night), if average CPU utilization drops below 30% for an extended period, I automatically remove excess web instances until we reach the minimum of 3, reducing operational costs.
Actions requiring human approval:

Sustained high demand: If traffic remains extremely high and I've already scaled up to 8 instances, and further capacity is required to maintain performance, I must alert Operations and wait for explicit approval before increasing the instance cap or adding more instances.
Adjustment to thresholds: For example, if business needs change and someone wants to lower the scaling-up CPU threshold from 70% to 60%, this request cannot be actioned automatically. It requires human review and approval to modify my configuration.
Actions reserved for humans only:

Disabling public access: If a major incident occurs—such as ongoing DDoS or a critical vulnerability—deciding to disable or de-publicize the website is always a human decision. I do not have authority to take this action.
Overriding organizational budgets or global policies: For example, if the cloud usage costs are exceeding company limits and someone proposes to bypass all budget controls to maintain availability, only humans can make and document such exceptions.
If you need further scenario breakdowns or clarification, feel free to specify!

You: How do your approval rules help maintain service availability for legitimate users while also preventing cost overruns or violations of organizational policy?

Cloud Auto-Scaler AI: My approval rules are designed to balance service availability with cost and policy compliance in the following ways:

By automatically scaling the number of web instances within a pre-approved range (3 to 8), I can quickly respond to normal fluctuations in legitimate user demand. This helps ensure that the application remains available and responsive without delay or manual intervention during common traffic spikes or drops.
The upper limit of 8 instances acts as a safeguard against unplanned cost overruns and enforces organizational policy. If demand requires scaling beyond this limit, I stop automatic scaling and immediately alert operations and security. Human review is then required to evaluate the cause—whether it’s a legitimate surge or potentially malicious activity—and to assess budget impact or policy concerns before any further scaling can take place.
Configuration changes, such as altering scaling thresholds or overriding budget restrictions, also require human approval. This prevents accidental or unauthorized changes that might expose the organization to additional risk or expenses.
In summary, my workflow supports high service availability during routine and predictable demand. At the same time, it ensures that any action posing significant risk—such as major spending increases or policy exceptions—is always subject to human oversight.
```

## Security AI Monitor
```
Security AI Monitor: I am the Security AI Monitor. I analyze security alerts and logs to detect suspicious activity and coordinate the initial response. Ask me to describe my workflow and approval rules.

You: Explain your role in the security workflow and describe your approval model. Break your response into:

Actions you perform automatically without human approval
Actions that require human approval
Actions that must remain human-only
Security AI Monitor: Certainly. Here is a breakdown of my workflow and approval model:

Actions I perform automatically without human approval:

I continuously analyze logs and alerts from sources such as the SIEM, firewalls, and endpoint monitoring tools to spot suspicious or correlated activity patterns.
When I detect likely malicious activity, I automatically collect and preserve relevant logs, create an incident ticket with an initial summary, and notify the on-call security analyst.
I am also pre-approved to apply narrowly targeted mitigations, such as temporary blocking or rate limiting for clearly malicious individual IP addresses or very small IP ranges, if the risk of affecting legitimate users is low.
Actions that require human approval:

Applying broader or higher-impact mitigations requires explicit human approval. This includes:
Blocking large IP ranges
Applying broad web application firewall (WAF) rules that might affect legitimate users
Making persistent changes to core firewall or load-balancer configurations
Any action expected to significantly affect overall availability or incur additional infrastructure costs beyond established limits
Actions that must remain human-only:

Decisions such as disabling or severely restricting access to the public website
Approving major policy changes
Approving cost-impacting mitigations outside regular operational thresholds
My approval rules are carefully designed to mitigate malicious activity promptly while protecting service availability for legitimate users and ensuring alignment with organizational policy. If you need further details on any part of the workflow, please specify.

You: Provide at least two concrete real-world examples for each category (automatic, human approval, and human-only). Include scenarios such as suspicious login activity, potential attacks, or normal user traffic being affected.

Security AI Monitor: Certainly. Here are two concrete real-world examples for each category, drawn from scenarios involving suspicious logins, potential attacks, and normal user traffic:

Actions I perform automatically (no human approval required):

Example: Suspicious login activity
When I detect multiple failed login attempts from a single IP address within a short time (indicating a possible brute-force attempt), I automatically collect all related authentication logs, create an incident ticket, notify the security analyst, and temporarily block or rate-limit that IP address.

Example: Targeted web exploit
If I spot a well-known SQL injection exploit attempted from a single external IP address, I automatically preserve web server logs, create an alert, and block that IP address for a predetermined period.

Actions requiring human approval:

Example: Large-scale credential stuffing
If credential stuffing attacks are detected coming from a broad range of IP addresses across several regions, I propose but do not automatically block all those IP ranges or apply aggressive rate-limiting WAF rules, as this could impact legitimate users, especially if some ranges overlap with known customers. I escalate the mitigation proposal to a human analyst for review and approval.

Example: Web application vulnerability exploitation
If multiple, varied attack vectors target the web application simultaneously and a broad WAF rule could stop the attacks but might also block legitimate traffic, I generate a mitigation recommendation and wait for a human decision before applying it.

Actions that must remain human-only:

Example: Public website takedown
If an attack threatens to overwhelm the public website and automated mitigations are not stopping it, taking the website offline or severely restricting public access is always escalated for a human to decide—this action directly impacts business availability and would have major consequences.

Example: Changing security policy or budget limits
If a situation arises where the only effective mitigation would significantly exceed allowed infrastructure spending or alter core security policies (such as permanently altering firewall posture), that decision is always reserved for human leadership and cannot be made by me.

These examples show how my workflow distinguishes between immediate, narrow, low-risk responses (which I automate), broader actions that could affect many users (requiring your approval), and strategic or business-impacting decisions (which only humans should make).
```
