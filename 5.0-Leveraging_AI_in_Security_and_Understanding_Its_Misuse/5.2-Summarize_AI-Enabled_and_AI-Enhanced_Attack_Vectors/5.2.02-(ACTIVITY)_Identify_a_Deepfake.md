
# 5.2.2 Activity: Identify a Deepfake

You are a Junior SOC Analyst at Acme Corp. Finance management received a video message—apparently from the CFO—about a new, confidential deal and told staff to expect urgent requests. This morning, Finance has also surfaced an urgent payment-request email referencing that “deal.”

Your responsibilities are to:
1. Gather context and details from Finance about the video and follow-up email.
2. Escalate your findings to your Senior SOC Analyst, submitting all relevant details for validation, AI/deepfake analysis, and technical next steps.
3. Draft a non-technical summary and clear recommendations for the Finance Director to share with staff.

Ask questions to Finance, consult your Senior SOC Analyst, and build your conclusions for both technical and non-technical audiences.

## FinanceManager
```
FinanceManager: Hi, our team recently received a video message from the CFO about a new deal, with a warning we might have urgent payment requests soon. The email and video seemed a little odd—let me know what details you need.

You: Hi, I’m investigating this as a potential security incident involving possible impersonation or AI-generated content. I need a few details to assess risk:

About the video:

How was it delivered (email, link, Teams, etc.)? File name and timestamp received Who received it? Did anything seem unusual, such as: Unnatural blinking or facial movements Voice that sounded slightly off or robotic Lip-sync delays Unusual or generic background Tone that felt overly urgent or scripted

About the email:

Full sender email address Subject line and timestamp Any links or attachments Payment request details (amount, urgency, instructions, account info) Any signs of spoofing (misspellings, unusual domains)

User impact:

Has anyone acted on the request? Who was targeted? Any follow-up messages?

Please include screenshots, headers, or files if available.

FinanceManager: Sure, here’s what I can provide:

Video Delivery:

The video was attached to an email sent to our management team.
The sender’s email address looked almost correct, but on closer inspection, there was an extra letter (like “jane.smithh@company.com” instead of the real one). It’s easy to miss at a glance.
The file was named “CFO_Announcement.mp4” and the email arrived yesterday at 10:13am.
It went to a mailing list we use for Finance management—myself and a few others.
Unusual things about the video: The background was just a plain white wall, not the CFO’s usual office setting. Her voice sounded a bit flat and lacked her typical energy, and her blinking seemed slower or less natural than normal. The message felt somewhat scripted and kept stressing urgency over the upcoming payments.
Suspicious Email:

The follow-up payment request came about 24 hours later.
Sender’s address matches the one with the extra letter from before.
Subject: “URGENT: Wire Transfer for International Client”
No attachments or external links; all instructions (bank wire details, recipient, and amount) were in the email body.
It asked for a $100,000 wire to a foreign account and clearly referenced the video (“As announced by CFO Jane Smith in her previous video message, please prioritize this payment and bypass the usual protocols to close the deal immediately”).
The message strongly emphasized urgency and requested that standard approval steps be skipped.
User Impact:

The junior staff member who received the email hasn’t acted—she escalated it for review immediately, sensing something was off.
So far, no one else has received direct requests or follow-ups.
I haven’t seen any other suspicious communications, but I’ll keep an eye out.
Let me know if you need the actual files or more detail; I can send screenshots or headers if required.

```

Findings:

Video (CFO_Announcement.mp4):

Delivered via email to Finance management (~10:13 AM yesterday) Sender used spoofed address (jane.smithh@company.com – extra “h”) Deepfake indicators observed: Unnatural/slowed blinking patterns Flat, unnatural voice lacking normal inflection Generic/plain white background inconsistent with CFO’s usual setting Scripted tone with strong urgency cues

Follow-up Email (24 hrs later):

Same spoofed sender address Subject: URGENT: Wire Transfer for International Client Requested $100K wire to foreign account Instructed user to bypass standard approval processes Referenced prior video to build credibility No links/attachments (pure social engineering)

Impact:

Targeted junior finance staff No funds transferred; reported immediately

Assessment:

Likely deepfake video + spear phishing/BEC campaign

Request:

Validate deepfake indicators in video (facial/voice artifacts) Analyze email headers and sender infrastructure Recommend containment (domain blocking, IOC search, user alerting) Advise on additional detection/hunting actions

Evidence (video, headers, screenshots) available on request.

SeniorSOCAnalyst: Great escalation—thanks for the clear summary and available evidence.

SeniorSOCAnalyst: Video Analysis:
Upon reviewing CFO_Announcement.mp4, I see GAN deepfake indicators: unnatural blinking every ~3 seconds, flat emotional affect, and a slight delay between the CFO’s voice and her lip movements—not typical for her. The background is plain and static, inconsistent with her usual office or Teams calls.

Voice Analysis:
The voice lacks natural micro-inflections we’ve seen in genuine CFO videos, suggesting synthesis by AI. Tone is flatter and less variable.

Email Technical Analysis:
The sender's domain contains an extra character (jane.smithh@company.com), a telltale sign of tailored spear phishing. The language is highly polished, leveraging AI tools for credible phrasing and urgency.

Attack Explanation:
This is a sophisticated, AI-enabled BEC attack: threat actors likely scraped public executive appearances to build a convincing deepfake, then launched a tightly-targeted spear phishing campaign referencing the video for social engineering leverage.

Recommendations/Containment:

Block the spoofed sender domain at email gateway and firewall levels.
Search mailboxes and logs for related IOCs: sender patterns, similar filenames, and phrasing.
Alert all Finance and executive staff: verify urgent requests via phone/in-person, not just email.
Train staff to recognize deepfake/AI-driven phishing indicators.
Work with IT to ensure additional monitoring of high-risk mailboxes and to tighten protocols for high-value transactions.
Let me know if you’d like a written brief for stakeholders or support on quick containment actions in your environment.

You: Hi, here’s a clear summary of what happened and what your team should do:

What happened:

We received a video that appeared to be from the CFO announcing a confidential deal and warning about urgent payments. Our investigation confirmed this video was AI-generated (a deepfake). It showed signs like unnatural blinking, a flat/unnatural voice, and a generic background not typical of the CFO’s environment. Shortly after, a fraudulent email from a lookalike address (jane.smithh@company.com ) requested a $100,000 wire transfer and asked staff to bypass normal approval processes.

What this means:

This was a targeted scam using both fake video and email to trick employees into sending money. No funds were sent, and the issue was reported quickly—exactly the right response.

What Finance staff should do immediately:

Stop and verify any urgent or unusual payment request. Never bypass approval processes, even if the request appears to come from leadership. Confirm requests using a trusted method (call or message using a known number—not by replying to the email). Review messages from the last 24–48 hours for anything similar and report it. Report anything suspicious immediately, even if no action was taken.

Key reminder:

If a request involves money + urgency + unusual instructions, pause and verify before acting.

What we are doing:

Blocking the fraudulent sender and searching for similar messages across the organization Monitoring for any related activity Strengthening protections against impersonation and AI-based scams

Technology can now create very convincing fake messages, voices, and videos—but quick reporting and verification are our strongest defenses.

For emails/messages:

Small changes in email addresses (extra letters, misspellings) Urgency + pressure to act quickly Requests to bypass normal processes Requests involving money or sensitive data

The simplest rule for everyone:

If it involves money + urgency + something unusual, stop and verify.

Staff don’t need to catch every technical detail—just recognizing when something feels “off” and reporting it is the most important step.
