
# 6.3.3 Organizational AI Policies

A clear corporate policy for artificial intelligence (AI) defines what is permitted, who is accountable, and how risks are controlled throughout the entire lifecycle of AI use. Since AI systems can process and transform large volumes of information in ways that are difficult to monitor, the policy must link business intent with specific rules for data handling, tools, and monitoring. The most effective policies translate enterprise risk into everyday actions that employees and vendors can easily follow, without requiring deep technical expertise.

## Sanctioned vs. Unsanctioned AI
In practice, ***sanctioned AI*** refers to tools and workflows that have been vetted and approved by the organization. These are configured to meet security and privacy requirements and are supported by the IT and legal teams. ***Unsanctioned AI***, often referred to as **shadow AI**, encompasses any AI tools that employees adopt independently, such as free web interfaces for large models or browser extensions that access external services. The goal of the policy is not only to discourage unsafe behavior but also to create safe, high-utility alternatives so that employees prefer using the approved options. Mature programs facilitate the use of sanctioned tools over unsanctioned ones, for example, by offering a searchable catalog of pre-approved AI assistants with single sign-on capabilities and clear usage guidance.

## Private vs. Public Models
***Public models*** are accessed as cloud services that are shared among multiple customers. While they offer speed and a wide range of capabilities, they also raise concerns about where prompts and outputs are stored, whether data might be used to enhance the provider's models, and how jurisdictional or sectoral regulations apply. ***Private models***, on the other hand, are deployed in a logically isolated environment, such as a dedicated tenant, a virtual private cloud, or on-premises infrastructure. They provide greater control over data residency, keys, logging, and tuning, although this comes with increased engineering and operational demands. Sound policy does not outright ban public models; instead, it establishes decision criteria. For low-risk use cases, such as brainstorming generic marketing data, a public model with a strong data-use guarantee may be acceptable. However, for high-risk scenarios, like processing sensitive health or financial records, the policy should prioritize private models that ensure explicit data isolation, customer-managed encryption keys, and contractual assurances that the provider will not train on the organization's data.

## Sensitive Data Governance
AI policy must align with the organization's data classification scheme. If the enterprise has already categorized information as public, internal, confidential, or restricted (or similar), those classifications should dictate the AI handling rules. For each classification, the policy should specify whether the data can be used for training, fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, or ad-hoc prompting, which model types are permitted, what masking, tokenization, or redaction is required, and what logging and retention limits apply. A common approach is to prohibit any restricted data from leaving the organization's trust boundary, require automatic redaction for confidential data before it is sent to a model, and allow internal data only with models that do not retain prompts for the provider's training. When the organization lacks a mature classification system, the AI policy can start with a simplified rule set, treating source code, credentials, customer personal data, and unreleased product information as restricted until a comprehensive scheme is established.
