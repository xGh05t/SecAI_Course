
# 6.3.6 Activity: Analyze an Organization's AI Structure

You have been appointed as an internal compliance consultant. Your task is to analyze your organization’s AI governance structure in preparation for a third-party compliance assessment. By interviewing stakeholders and reviewing provided information, identify strengths, gaps, and risks in the organization’s AI compliance posture, with attention to:

- Governance and accountability structures
- Model inventory/registration
- Data governance and privacy controls
- Risk assessment procedures
- Documentation and audit readiness
- Model fairness and performance management

A practical approach is to start with the **AI Governance & Engineering Lead** to understand overall governance, inventory status, and technical risk management practices, then consult the **Data Privacy Lead** for data governance, consent, minimization, and privacy impact assessments. You may still interact with resources in any order and ask follow-up questions as needed.

When you are ready to submit your analysis, open the **Chief AI Compliance Officer’s** chat to receive summary questions. Your responses to these questions, along with your inquiry process, will be evaluated using the established rubric.

## AI Governance & Engineering Lead
```
AI Governance & Engineering Lead: Welcome. I can answer questions about our AI governance program, model inventory, system architecture, validation and testing practices, and how we integrate AI into applications.

You: I’d like to understand your AI governance and technical risk management practices at both a structural and operational level.

Can you describe the organization’s AI governance framework, including roles, responsibilities, and how accountability is enforced across AI systems? Additionally, do you maintain a centralized model inventory, and if so, what type of metadata is tracked (e.g., model purpose, ownership, risk classification, training data sources, deployment status)?

From a risk perspective, how are AI systems evaluated and categorized (e.g., high vs. low risk), and what frameworks or standards guide this process?

I’m also interested in your model lifecycle controls. What validation and testing procedures are performed before deployment (e.g., performance, adversarial, bias testing), and how are models monitored post-deployment for drift, degradation, or anomalous behavior? Are there defined thresholds or review cycles in place?

Finally, what documentation is maintained to support audit readiness (e.g., model cards, risk assessments, decision logs), and how do you address fairness, bias, and explainability throughout the lifecycle?

AI Governance & Engineering Lead: Thank you for your comprehensive set of questions—these touch on both our structural governance as well as our operational and technical controls for AI.

AI Governance Framework (Roles, Responsibilities, Accountability):

At the structural level, we have an AI Oversight Committee that reviews major AI risk decisions on a quarterly basis. The committee includes members from technology, compliance, risk, and business leadership. Responsibilities for each AI project are defined within project charters, with clear assignment of model ownership (typically to the technical lead or system owner), business owners, and risk/compliance monitors. Accountability is enforced through reporting lines and review protocols—project teams must submit documentation for oversight at key project milestones.

Model Inventory & Metadata Tracked:

We do maintain a centralized AI model inventory. The inventory was last updated nine months ago; some newer models may not yet be listed. Metadata tracked includes model name, business purpose, model owner, risk classification (high/medium/low), training data source, deployment environment, and current deployment status.

AI Risk Evaluation & Categorization:

Models are categorized by risk level (e.g., high, medium, low) based on a combination of their business impact, level of autonomy, data sensitivity, and potential for unintended consequences. Our formal AI policy aligns with ISO 42001 and covers accountability, risk, and lifecycle management.

Model Lifecycle Controls (Validation, Testing, Monitoring):

All high-risk AI models are required to undergo robustness and fairness testing prior to deployment. This includes performance testing, adversarial robustness checks, and bias assessments.
Validation and testing results are documented and retained for one year after each model deployment.
For post-deployment monitoring, no automated post-market drift monitoring is currently in place for legacy models; only manual spot checks occur.
Documentation for Audit Readiness:

Deployment logs exist but are retained for only three months. Validation/testing documentation, project risk assessments, and decision logs are maintained in our central repository to support audit readiness. The last internal audit flagged gaps in impact assessments for two newly deployed AI tools.

Fairness, Bias, and Explainability:

Addressing fairness and bias is integral to our validation process—especially for high-risk models. Model explainability is assessed during validation, and performance metrics are part of model cards prepared post-validation. Documentation is aligned to facilitate transparency for both internal and external audits.

I can provide more detail on any specific area—for example, our risk classification methodology, or the metadata tracking fields—if you’d like to dive deeper. Let me know your preferences.

You: Thank you—this is very helpful. I’d like to clarify a few areas around enforcement, monitoring, and integration.

How are gaps in the model inventory (such as newly deployed models not yet registered) tracked and remediated, and is there a defined process or SLA for keeping the inventory current?

Regarding post-deployment monitoring, are there plans to implement automated drift detection or continuous monitoring, and how are risks managed in the absence of such controls for legacy systems?

You mentioned prior audit findings related to missing impact assessments—how are these findings tracked, remediated, and validated to ensure closure?

Finally, how are governance processes (inventory, risk classification, validation results) integrated across systems to support real-time oversight and audit reporting?

AI Governance & Engineering Lead: Thank you for your follow-up. Let me address each point in detail:

1. Tracking and Remediating Model Inventory Gaps: Currently, updates to our model inventory rely on periodic reviews coordinated by the AI Oversight Committee and self-reporting by project teams at key deployment milestones. There is no formal SLA in place for real-time updates to the inventory, which means some models—especially those deployed outside of major project cycles—may experience a delay before being registered. Gaps identified during quarterly reviews are documented and assigned to system owners for prompt update. The Committee follows up on outstanding items in subsequent sessions, but a more structured, automated feed from deployment systems is under evaluation.

2. Plans for Automated Drift and Continuous Monitoring: At present, no automated post-market drift monitoring is in place for legacy models—only manual spot checks are performed. There is an engineering initiative underway to integrate automated drift detection tools into our standardized deployment pipelines for all new high-risk models. For legacy models, risk is managed by focusing manual reviews on those used for significant business processes and by applying stricter review protocols for any models flagged as outliers in periodic audit samples.
```

## Data Privacy Lead
```
Data Privacy Lead: I can provide details on data privacy controls, personal data handling, minimization, and legal/data protection compliance.

You: I’d like to review how data governance and privacy controls are implemented across AI systems.

Can you describe the policies and technical controls in place to ensure data minimization and appropriate data collection for AI use cases? How is user consent obtained, recorded, and enforced, and how do you ensure compliance with applicable regulations such as GDPR or CCPA?

Additionally, how does the organization identify and protect sensitive or regulated data used in training and inference processes?

From a risk and compliance standpoint, are Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) or similar evaluations conducted for AI systems, and at what stages in the lifecycle?

Finally, what controls exist for data retention, deletion, and anonymization, and how are privacy incidents (e.g., data breaches involving AI systems) detected, reported, and mitigated?

Data Privacy Lead: Certainly, let me address each of your inquiries point by point as they relate to data privacy and compliance within our AI systems:

Data Minimization and Appropriate Data Collection

We have a policy requiring that all production AI models undergo periodic reviews for data minimization and purpose limitation. This means only data strictly necessary for the defined business purpose is collected and retained, and data collection workflows are routinely audited to confirm alignment with this principle.
For every new AI use case, a data inventory and classification exercise is conducted to assess data sources, categories, and their intended use.
User Consent and Compliance

Explicit consent is required before using personal data in AI solutions. This consent is typically obtained through dedicated forms or digital interfaces, stored within our consent management platform, and programmatically enforced before any data processing begins.
Compliance with global privacy regulations, such as GDPR and CCPA, is achieved by embedding privacy requirements into our design and review processes, including legal reviews and privacy-by-design workshops.
Identification and Protection of Sensitive or Regulated Data

Restricted (e.g., sensitive personal) data is not permitted for AI model training; confidential data must be redacted prior to use.
Automated data discovery tools scan datasets before ingestion to flag sensitive information. Where confidential or regulated data is required for business needs, it is anonymized or pseudonymized, and additional access controls are implemented.
Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)

DPIAs or similar assessments are performed prior to deployment of any AI system processing personal or sensitive data, and are updated when major design or use case changes occur.
Routine compliance audits check for the presence and currency of DPIAs.
Data Retention, Deletion, and Anonymization Controls

We enforce strict retention schedules—no personal data is retained longer than necessary, and automated scripts support timely deletion.
Anonymization and pseudonymization techniques are applied when data is used for model training or analytics.
Access to deletion and retention functions is monitored and restricted to privileged staff.
Privacy Incident Detection and Response

Automated monitoring systems flag anomalous data access or usage patterns associated with AI platforms.
Should a privacy breach occur, we have incident response protocols mandating prompt reporting, impact assessment, notification (in accordance with regulations), and remediation.
```

## Chief AI Compliance Officer
```
Chief AI Compliance Officer: Thank you for completing your analysis. Please answer the following questions as thoroughly as possible:

Governance Structure Analysis: How effective is the organization's governance and accountability structure for managing AI compliance? What strengths or weaknesses did you identify?

Policy & Documentation Awareness: Summarize the organization’s AI-related policies and documentation. Are there any notable gaps or areas for improvement?

Data Governance & Privacy: Evaluate the organization’s approach to data governance, data minimization, and privacy controls. What are the major concerns or strengths?

Risk & Testing Review: How are AI model risks assessed and managed (including validation/testing, monitoring)? Where are deficiencies present?

Gap Identification & Recommendations: List the key compliance gaps and risks you’ve discovered. What actionable recommendations would you make to improve the organization’s AI compliance posture?

You: The organization’s AI governance structure is well-defined but operationally immature. A key strength is the AI Oversight Committee, which provides cross-functional accountability and structured review of AI risk. Roles and responsibilities are clearly assigned through project charters. However, governance enforcement relies heavily on manual reporting, periodic reviews, and self-reporting, which limits real-time oversight. Additionally, governance data is fragmented across multiple systems, reducing efficiency and traceability.

The organization maintains strong AI policies aligned with ISO/IEC 42001 and supports them with documentation such as model inventories, risk assessments, validation results, and model cards. Privacy policies are also well-established, including consent management, DPIAs, and data minimization practices. However, execution gaps exist: the model inventory is outdated and incomplete, deployment logs are retained for only three months, validation records for one year, and prior audits identified missing impact assessments—indicating inconsistent enforcement.

Data governance and privacy controls are a major strength. The organization enforces data minimization, requires explicit consent, conducts DPIAs, and uses automated tools for sensitive data detection and anonymization. Retention and deletion controls are also in place. The main concern is that these controls depend on full governance coverage—if AI systems are not captured in the inventory, they may bypass these protections.

AI risk is assessed through structured classification based on impact, autonomy, and data sensitivity. High-risk models undergo pre-deployment testing, including performance, robustness, and bias assessments, with documented results. However, post-deployment monitoring is weak. There is no automated drift detection for legacy models, and monitoring relies on manual spot checks. Governance systems are also not fully integrated, requiring manual reconciliation for oversight.

Key gaps include: incomplete model inventory (shadow AI risk), lack of automated monitoring, fragmented governance systems, limited documentation retention, and inconsistent execution of impact assessments. These create regulatory, operational, and audit risks.

To improve compliance, the organization should implement automated model inventory updates tied to deployment pipelines, deploy continuous monitoring for drift and bias, extend log and documentation retention, integrate governance systems into unified reporting dashboards, and strengthen validation of audit remediation efforts. Aligning privacy controls with governance processes will ensure full coverage across all AI systems.
```
