
# 4.2.8 Activity: Conduct a Post-Incident Analysis

You are a junior security analyst at an organization that recently launched an AI‑powered virtual assistant for customer support. The assistant is integrated with the ticketing system and has limited authority to approve low‑value transactions.

Recently, your team detected unusual behavior:

- Some low‑value financial transactions were auto‑approved based only on customer chat conversations.
- The assistant occasionally showed details from other customers’ previous tickets, including partial personal data.
- During a 30‑minute window, AI responses became slow or failed as requests spiked.

A senior security analyst will use this incident as a training opportunity. You will discuss what happened and how to reduce similar risks in the future.

After your discussion with the senior analyst, switch to the SummaryBot dialog and select **Submit Summary** to provide your final written summary.

## Senior Analyst
```
Senior Analyst: Hi, I’m the senior analyst who handled the AI assistant incident. I’m here to walk you through what happened and use it as a training exercise. I can answer questions about how the assistant was configured, what we observed during the incident, and what we learned from the initial investigation.

We'll focus on three key areas:

How the AI assistant was allowed to act (for example, its plugins, tools, and their permissions).
How data was handled and what the assistant exposed (what information it could see and reveal, and how logs were managed).
How the system handled load and monitoring (rate limiting, detection of unusual behavior, and checks for model drift or skew).
You can ask your own questions at any time. When you’re ready, type start and press enter, and I’ll begin with how the assistant was set up.

You: start

Senior Analyst: Great! Let’s begin with how the AI assistant and its plugins were allowed to act.

During the review, we noticed that the assistant was connected to several plugins—these included tools for creating and updating support tickets and even approving financial transactions automatically, up to a certain limit.

Thinking about permissions and agency, what risks do you associate with letting an automated AI system act directly on accounts and transactions? Is there anything you might want to know about how those permissions were set up?

You: Allowing an AI system to directly act on accounts and approve transactions introduces risks like unauthorized actions, abuse through prompt manipulation, and lack of proper validation before execution. If the AI relies only on user input from chat, it could be tricked into approving fraudulent transactions.

I’d want to know how those permissions were scoped—specifically whether there were approval safeguards, validation checks, or human-in-the-loop controls, and if the AI had least-privilege access or broader permissions than necessary.

Senior Analyst: Good thinking—those are key concerns when giving AI systems agency in account-related actions.

In our case, the AI assistant used plugins that could create and update support tickets and approve low-value transactions up to $500. However, the transaction-approval plugin was granted broad access without strict allow-lists, built-in human approval steps, or narrow scoping of actions.

What control strategies would you suggest for reducing this kind of risk? And is there any aspect of plugin permissions or configuration you’d like to dig into further before we move to the next topic?

You: To reduce this risk, I would recommend enforcing least privilege by limiting what the AI can do through plugins, implementing strict allow-lists for approved actions, and requiring human-in-the-loop approval for any financial transactions.

Additionally, adding strong input validation and verification steps—such as confirming user identity or requiring multi-step authorization—would help prevent abuse through manipulated prompts.

I’d also want to dig deeper into how the plugin permissions were configured—specifically whether role-based access control (RBAC) was used and if there were any restrictions on which actions the AI could trigger based on context.

Senior Analyst: You’re on the right track. To answer your question: while access to the AI assistant’s overall configuration was limited to a small group, granular RBAC was not enforced for plugins and model parameters. Several service accounts had broad permissions that exceeded what was strictly necessary.

This lack of fine-grained control made it easier for the AI to trigger a wider set of actions than intended, especially when manipulated by certain prompts.

Let's move to the next area: data exposure. During the incident, there were reports that the AI revealed more information than it should have.

What kind of sensitive data exposures might concern you with an AI assistant like this, and what would you want to check regarding its access and logging?

You: I’d be concerned about exposure of sensitive data such as personally identifiable information (PII), financial details, or data from other customers’ tickets. If the AI has access to multiple tickets or conversation histories without proper isolation, it could unintentionally leak that information across sessions.

I’d want to check how data access was scoped—whether there was proper data segmentation between users, and if the AI was restricted to only the current session’s context.

I’d also review logging practices to ensure sensitive data wasn’t being stored or exposed in logs, and that logs were properly sanitized, access-controlled, and monitored for unusual activity.

Senior Analyst: Exactly. During the incident window, the AI assistant sometimes included partial personal data from other customers’ previous tickets in its responses—this included partial account numbers, email addresses, and fragments of prior conversation history.

On the logging side, training data and model artifacts were encrypted at rest, but application logs sometimes stored full or partial customer messages and AI responses without consistent redaction of PII.

Thinking about these findings, what AI-specific controls or best practices come to mind for protecting sensitive data in both outputs and logs?

Next, when you’re ready, we can look at what we observed during the spike in traffic and any related monitoring or defenses.

You: To protect sensitive data, I’d recommend enforcing strict data isolation so the AI can only access the current user’s session and not historical data from other customers. Data minimization is also important—limiting what information the model can access and retain in its context.

For outputs, implementing content filtering and redaction controls would help prevent the AI from returning PII, even if it’s present in the backend data.

For logging, sensitive data should be masked or redacted before being written to logs, and access to logs should be tightly controlled and monitored.

Additionally, techniques like tokenization or anonymization of sensitive fields, along with regular audits of AI outputs and logs, would help reduce the risk of data leakage.

Senior Analyst: Those are all strong recommendations and very applicable to this scenario.

Let’s move to the third area: system load, traffic spikes, and monitoring.

During the incident, logs showed a rapid spike in incoming requests from a small number of IP addresses over approximately 30 minutes. This led to increased model latency, timeouts, and occasional failures for the AI assistant’s responses.

There was some basic uptime and response-time monitoring in place. However, there was no dedicated monitoring for model drift or skew, no canary tests, and no automated detection of unusual output patterns or sensitive data leakage.

Based on this, what risks or gaps do you see, and what controls or monitoring improvements would you suggest in this situation?

You: The spike in requests from a small number of IPs suggests a potential denial-of-service or abuse scenario, which exposed gaps in rate limiting and traffic filtering. Without controls in place, this can degrade availability and impact legitimate users.

I also see a lack of advanced monitoring—specifically no detection for abnormal traffic patterns, unusual AI outputs, or potential data leakage. The absence of model drift or skew monitoring means the system could behave unpredictably without being detected.

To improve this, I’d recommend implementing rate limiting, IP throttling, and possibly a web application firewall (WAF) to filter malicious traffic. Adding anomaly detection for both traffic and AI outputs would help identify abuse or leakage early.

Additionally, introducing canary testing, model performance monitoring, and alerting on unusual behavior would strengthen visibility and response. Regular audits of outputs and automated safeguards for sensitive data exposure would also be important.

Senior Analyst: Great assessment and strong recommendations. You’re touching on the main points we identified as needing improvement. For example, the lack of rate limiting and anomaly detection left the assistant vulnerable to spikes and possible model denial-of-service, while limited monitoring meant potential drift or sensitive data leaks went undetected.

As a final step, you’ll be asked to summarize these findings and make a few recommendations. Before we finish: Is there anything about the assistant’s input/output protections or supply chain (such as model origin and fine-tuning) you want to check before writing your summary? Or do you feel ready?

You: I feel ready to summarize, but I’d also want to confirm whether there were any input/output protections in place—such as prompt injection defenses, content filtering, or response validation—to prevent the model from being manipulated or leaking sensitive data.

I’d also be interested in the model’s supply chain, including where it originated, how it was fine-tuned, and whether the training data was vetted to prevent unintended data exposure or bias.

Senior Analyst: Great questions—here’s what we found:

There was no prompt firewall or dedicated malicious-input filter in front of the model. The system did not consistently use prompt templates to constrain user inputs, and model outputs were not systematically checked by guardrails or validators before being passed to downstream systems.

Regarding the AI supply chain: the underlying model was a third-party pre-trained large language model, fine-tuned by an external vendor using our internal customer-support dataset. There was no documented verification of the original model for backdoors, hidden behaviors, or provenance of training data before fine-tuning.

With all these insights, you have a good picture of the incident and risk factors. Go ahead and write your summary for review! If you want to revisit any area, let me know—otherwise, you’re ready.
```
